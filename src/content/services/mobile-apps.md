Shipping a mobile app is not the hard part. Shipping the fourth release is. Apps live under constraints a website never faces: a review process between you and your users, two platforms with different conventions, devices that go offline mid-action, and old versions that stay installed for years whether you support them or not.

Most of what determines whether an app is maintainable is decided before the first screen is built.

## Where this usually goes wrong

**Cross-platform chosen as a belief.** Cross-platform is right for most products and wrong for some. Heavy media processing, deep OS integration or demanding real-time interaction can justify native. Picking either as a default rather than a judgement is what produces expensive rewrites.

**Offline treated as an error state.** Mobile networks fail mid-action constantly. An app that assumes connectivity and shows a spinner when it is wrong feels broken even when nothing is. Sync and conflict resolution are architecture, not polish.

**Release process discovered late.** Store review, staged rollouts, versioning, forced-upgrade paths and crash triage arrive as a surprise in the final week rather than as part of the plan.

**Push used as a broadcast channel.** Notifications sent because there is a campaign rather than because something happened that this user cares about — the fastest way to get permission revoked permanently.

**No plan for old versions.** Users do not all update. An API that assumes they have is an outage waiting for a specific Tuesday.

## How we run it

The platform decision comes first and is argued explicitly against your product: what it does, what it integrates with, what performance it needs, and what your team can maintain afterwards. Cross-platform where it saves money without costing feel; native where the product genuinely demands it.

Architecture is designed around state and sync rather than screens — what happens when the network drops mid-write, what wins in a conflict, what the app can do with no connection at all.

Release engineering is set up early: build pipelines, staged rollout, crash reporting and a forced-upgrade path that exists before you need it. The first release is a rehearsal of every release after it.

Lifecycle messaging is designed as product behaviour rather than a marketing surface — triggered by real events, with a permission cost consciously weighed each time.

## The part most agencies cannot do

Apps have an acquisition problem that websites do not: the store listing is a ranking surface, install attribution is genuinely difficult, and the gap between an ad tap and an activated user crosses the app store, the onboarding flow and the product itself. Those belong to three different suppliers in most setups, and nobody owns the number end to end.

We hold both ends. The team running acquisition is the team that can change onboarding, instrument the funnel properly, and adjust the store listing — so a drop-off between install and activation is a fixable product problem rather than a dispute about whose fault it is.

## Who this is for

An app is right when the product genuinely needs the device — offline use, notifications people want, camera or location, or a habit worth an icon on a home screen. When those are absent, a fast responsive site usually reaches more people for less money, and we will recommend that instead.

It suits companies prepared to fund the second year. Apps are not a project with an end date; they are a release cadence, and one shipped without a maintenance budget degrades visibly.

If the goal is reach rather than depth, start with [Websites](/services/websites). The store is a distribution channel with a real cost of entry.
