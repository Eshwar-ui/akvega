Design work fails in the gap between the file and the build. The flows are considered, the screens are right, and then engineering hits the states nobody drew — the empty one, the error, the row with forty characters in a field designed for twelve, the slow network. Those get invented under time pressure, and the shipped product is the sum of those inventions.

Design that survives is design that anticipated them, expressed in a form the build can consume directly.

## Where this usually goes wrong

**Happy paths only.** A flow designed for the user who has data, permissions and connectivity. Empty, loading, error, partial and permission-denied states are most of what people actually encounter in a working product, and they are usually undrawn.

**Research that confirms.** Interviews run to validate a decision already made. The questions are leading, the sample is friendly, and the output is a slide saying users liked it.

**Screens instead of systems.** Forty artboards with subtly different button padding. Engineering picks one interpretation per screen and the product drifts before launch.

**Handoff as a folder.** Specs as images with redlines rather than tokens and components. Every value gets retyped, and every retyped value is a chance to be wrong.

**Prototypes that hide the hard part.** A polished click-through of the ideal path, tested with people who were told what to do, proving only that a demo works.

**No accessibility until audit.** Contrast, focus order, target size and semantics treated as a compliance pass at the end, when they are structural and cheap to get right early.

## How we run it

Research sized to the decision it informs — enough to find out where people actually get stuck, run so it can produce an unwelcome answer.

Flows before screens, including the states that are not the happy path. If a state exists in the system it gets designed, because otherwise it gets improvised.

Interface systems rather than page designs: components, tokens, spacing and type scales, with the rules that make a new screen a composition rather than a fresh design problem.

Prototypes built to test the risky part — the step we think will fail, at enough fidelity that the test tells us something.

Handoff as tokens and components in the formats engineering consumes, with accessibility built into the components so it does not have to be retrofitted screen by screen.

## What you get

- **Prototypes** — built to test the part most likely to be wrong.

## The part most agencies cannot do

A design studio hands over a system and finds out how it fared when the product ships. Whatever engineering could not build in the time available was silently substituted, and the studio was not in the room to trade one thing for another.

We hold both ends. The people designing the system are working alongside the people building it, so the substitutions are decisions rather than surprises — and the design system exists in the codebase as components, which is the only place it can actually be enforced.

It also connects design to demand. When the same studio runs acquisition, the drop-off in a funnel is a design brief with evidence attached rather than a suspicion. See [Mobile apps](/services/mobile-apps) and [Websites](/services/websites) for where these systems get built.

## Who this is for

Design work of this kind suits products with enough usage to have real problems — where people are dropping out somewhere specific and nobody can say why. Very early products are usually better served by shipping a narrower thing and watching what happens.

It also needs a build capacity to hand to. A design system with nobody to implement it becomes an expensive archive, which is the main reason we would rather hold both ends — see [Mobile apps](/services/mobile-apps).

If the problem is that nobody arrives rather than that nobody converts, this is the wrong service. That is an acquisition problem.
