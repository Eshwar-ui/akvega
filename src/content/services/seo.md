Search stopped being one channel. A query can now resolve in an organic result, in an AI Overview that never earns a click, or inside ChatGPT and Perplexity where your brand is either cited or absent. These are not three separate disciplines with three separate retainers. They are the same underlying problem — whether a machine can find your content, understand what it says, and trust you enough to repeat it.

We run them as one programme because the work overlaps almost completely. Clean information architecture helps a crawler and an LLM equally. Structured data that resolves your entity properly improves both a rich result and the chance an answer engine names you. Splitting this across vendors mostly buys you two invoices and a coordination problem.

## Where this usually goes wrong

The common failure is an audit nobody can act on. An agency delivers a spreadsheet of 400 issues, hands it to a development team that did not scope the work, and the findings sit in a backlog until the next audit re-discovers them. The recommendations were probably correct. They were also never going to ship.

The second failure is content written for a keyword rather than a question. It ranks for a while, then an AI Overview answers the question directly above it and the traffic quietly halves. Pages that survive that shift are the ones that answer something specific, completely, in a structure a machine can extract.

The third is technical debt that no amount of content can outrun — render-blocking JavaScript, pages that need a client-side framework to show their own text, canonical tags pointing at the wrong URL. Search engines render JavaScript. Most social scrapers and several AI crawlers do not.

## How we run it

We start by establishing what is actually indexed and what is merely published — those are different numbers, and the gap is usually where the problem lives. From there the work splits into three tracks that run in parallel rather than in sequence.

**Technical.** Crawlability, indexability, render path, Core Web Vitals, canonical and sitemap integrity, and the status codes your server actually returns rather than the ones it is supposed to. We fix these in the codebase rather than describing them in a document, which is the part that makes the difference.

**Entity and structure.** Schema markup that resolves your organisation, services and pages into a connected graph rather than a scatter of disconnected snippets. This is the layer answer engines lean on hardest, and the one most sites leave empty.

**Answer coverage.** Mapping the questions your buyers actually ask, then building pages that answer them completely enough to be quotable. We track which engines cite you and which do not, and treat an uncited answer as a content gap rather than a mystery.

## What you get

- **Technical audits** — findings tied to specific files and templates, prioritised by what moves indexing rather than by what scores badly in a tool.
- **Content architecture** — the page and internal-linking structure that decides which of your URLs is allowed to rank for what.
- **Schema and entities** — structured data implemented and validated, not recommended.
- **AI answer coverage** — which questions you are cited on across the major answer engines, and what is missing.

## The part most agencies cannot do

Almost every SEO recommendation is a development ticket. Change the heading structure. Fix the render path. Add structured data. Restructure these URLs and redirect the old ones. An agency that only advises has to hand every one of those to someone else, and the handoff is where the programme dies.

We hold both ends. The people finding the problem are the people who ship the fix, which means the gap between "we found this" and "this is live" is a deploy rather than a quarter. If you already have an in-house engineering team, we work the other way and embed with them — but the work still lands rather than queuing.

That connection runs the other direction too. If we are also building the site, search is a constraint from the first commit rather than a retrofit. See [Websites](/services/websites) for how that works in practice.
