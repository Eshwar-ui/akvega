Search advertising is the one channel where demand already exists and you are only deciding what it costs to meet it. That makes it unusually honest — and unusually easy to waste money on, because a badly structured account can spend its entire budget looking productive.

The account is not the point. The point is what a click is worth to you and what you paid for it. Everything below follows from that.

## Where this usually goes wrong

**Optimising to the wrong number.** Conversions in the Ads interface are whatever the tag was told to count. If that tag fires on a thank-you page nobody qualified, on a scroll depth, or on a form submission that includes every bot that found the endpoint, the platform will faithfully buy you more of it. Automated bidding makes this worse rather than better: it optimises hard toward the target it was given, so a wrong target compounds.

**Match types treated as a formality.** Broad match with an underspecified negative list will find you queries you would never have chosen and charge you for them. The fix is not avoiding broad match — it is reading the search terms report weekly and acting on it.

**Landing pages nobody owns.** The campaign is agency work; the page is somebody else's backlog. So traffic lands on a generic page, converts poorly, and the account gets blamed for it. This is the most common reason a technically competent account underperforms.

**Brand spend counted as growth.** Bidding on your own name is often worth doing, but it converts at a rate no other campaign can match and it will flatter any blended number it is averaged into. Report it separately or it hides everything.

## How we run it

We start with measurement, because until conversion tracking is trustworthy every other decision is guesswork with a confident interface on top. That means auditing what fires, when, and whether the values attached to it reflect anything real.

Then structure: campaigns organised around intent and margin rather than around how the account happened to grow, so budget can move toward what earns without a rebuild first.

Then the query layer — intent mapping, negatives maintained as an ongoing job rather than a launch task, and keyword sets that reflect how buyers actually search rather than how the product is described internally.

Then the page. This is where the two tracks meet.

## What you get

- **Account restructure** — campaigns rebuilt around intent and profitability, with a clear reason each one exists.

- **Conversion tracking** — implemented and verified end to end, so the number the platform optimises toward is one you would defend.

## The part most agencies cannot do

A media agency can tell you the landing page is the bottleneck. It usually cannot fix it. So the recommendation becomes a ticket, the ticket becomes a quarter, and the account keeps buying clicks for a page nobody is happy with.

We hold both ends. The team buying the traffic is the team shipping the page it arrives on, which makes a landing page test a deploy rather than a negotiation. Page speed, form behaviour, tracking and copy are all in scope, because they are all part of what the click costs.

That changes what is worth testing. When building a variant is cheap, you can test structural ideas rather than button colours — a different offer, a different proof, a different form length. ## Who this is for

Paid search works when demand already exists and someone is typing for it. If nobody is searching for what you sell, the account cannot manufacture that — [Meta Ads](/services/meta-ads) or organic demand building is the honest route, and we will say so before taking the budget.

It also needs unit economics you can state. Not perfectly, but well enough to say what a lead is worth. Without that, every optimisation is a preference rather than a decision.

Very small budgets are worth being blunt about: below a certain daily spend there is not enough data for automated bidding to learn, and management fees eat the account.
