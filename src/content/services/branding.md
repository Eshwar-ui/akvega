Most identity work is judged on a presentation and then quietly abandoned in production. The logo survives. The rest — the type scale, the spacing logic, the tone, the rules about what the brand does not do — lasts until the first campaign that needs something the guidelines did not anticipate, and after that every team improvises.

A brand system is not a document. It is the set of decisions that hold up when a performance marketer needs a static ad by Thursday and a developer needs a component that was never designed.

## Where this usually goes wrong

**Guidelines written for a pitch, not for use.** Fifty pages of mood and three pages of rules. Beautiful, and useless at the moment somebody needs to know the minimum size of the mark on a mobile ad.

**No handoff into code.** Colour and type defined as swatches in a PDF rather than as tokens a front-end can consume means every implementation is an interpretation. Two years later there are four blues in production and nobody can say which is correct.

**Voice defined by adjectives.** "Confident, human, bold" describes almost every company and constrains nothing. Voice becomes usable when it says what you would never write, and shows the same sentence right and wrong.

**A system that only covers marketing.** Identity designed for campaigns and launch assets, with nothing to say about product interface, empty states, error messages or long-form documentation — which is where customers actually spend their time.

**Rebrand as a substitute for positioning.** New visual language applied to an unchanged and unclear proposition. It looks different and communicates the same confusion.

## How we run it

Strategy first, and only as much of it as changes a decision. What the company does, who it is for, what it claims that a competitor could not truthfully copy, and what that implies for tone and behaviour.

Then the visual system, designed against real applications rather than abstract compositions — an ad, a landing page, an interface, an email, the smallest and least glamorous surfaces included. A system that works there will work in a presentation. The reverse is not reliable.

Then the rules, written to be used: what to do, what not to do, and the specific edge cases people hit. Voice documented with real examples rather than adjectives.

Then the handoff. Tokens, components and assets in the formats the people who will actually implement this can consume — because that is the step where identity work either becomes real or becomes a PDF.

## What you get

- **Brand strategy** — the positioning and the claim, only as far as it changes design decisions.
- **Visual identity** — mark, type, colour, layout and motion, designed against real surfaces.
- **Brand guidelines** — usable rules and edge cases, not a mood presentation.
- **Naming and voice** — how the brand sounds, with examples of right and wrong.

## The part most agencies cannot do

Identity systems degrade at the point of implementation, and the studio that designed the system is usually not in the room when that happens. Someone builds the site, someone else builds the app, a media team makes the ads, and each interprets the guidelines slightly differently. Three years later the brand is whatever survived.

We hold both ends. The same studio can take the system into the site, the product interface and the campaign creative, delivered as design tokens and components rather than as reference images. That is what stops drift — not stricter guidelines, but fewer handoffs.

## Who this is for

Brand work is worth doing when the current identity is actively costing you — inconsistent across channels, unusable in product, or saying something you have outgrown. It is not worth doing as a substitute for a positioning decision nobody wants to make.

If you cannot yet say what you claim that a competitor could not truthfully copy, that is the work, and no visual system will stand in for it. We would rather spend the first phase there.

It also suits companies about to build something. An identity delivered just before a site or product build lands in production while the reasoning is still fresh — see [Websites](/services/websites).
