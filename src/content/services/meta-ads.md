On Meta, the creative is the targeting. Audience controls matter far less than they once did, and the delivery system will find the people who respond to a given piece of work. Which means the constraint on performance is rarely the media plan — it is how many genuinely different creative ideas you can put into the system, and how quickly you can tell which ones are working.

That is a production problem before it is a media problem. Most accounts stall because they run out of things to test, not because the buying was wrong.

## Where this usually goes wrong

**Creative and media on different clocks.** A media team spots a winning angle on Tuesday. The creative team is booked until the end of the month. By the time the follow-up lands, the original has fatigued and the insight has expired. This is the largest tax on paid social performance and it is entirely structural.

**Testing variations instead of ideas.** Five versions of the same concept with a different headline tell you very little. Five genuinely different arguments for why someone should care tell you what your market responds to.

**Reading attribution literally.** Platform-reported return is a model, not a measurement, and it is a model with an interest in the answer. Treating it as ground truth produces budget decisions that look excellent in the dashboard and never appear in revenue.

**Prospecting judged by retargeting standards.** Retargeting converts well because the demand already existed. Holding cold audiences to the same efficiency target quietly switches off the only campaigns building future demand.

## How we run it

Creative testing and media buying sit in the same team on the same cycle. When something proves itself, budget moves that week and the next round of production is briefed against what it revealed — not against a calendar set a quarter ago.

We structure accounts so learning is not destroyed every time something changes: enough consolidation for delivery to exit the learning phase, enough separation that prospecting, retargeting and retention can each be judged on their own terms.

Measurement is its own workstream. Platform numbers are useful for deciding which creative beats which creative. They are not sufficient for deciding what the channel is worth. We set up tracking to answer both questions and stay explicit about which one any given number is answering.

## The part most agencies cannot do

The bottleneck on Meta is production throughput, and most media agencies outsource production. That is why "we found a winner" so often arrives with a three-week wait attached.

We hold both ends. Design and build sit in the same studio as the buying, so a landing experience, a new format, or a page built for one campaign is work we schedule rather than work we request. If the winning angle needs a page that does not exist yet, that page can exist.

This is also where paid social stops being isolated from everything else you own. The creative language, the site and the product it points to can be one system — see [Branding](/services/branding) and [Product design](/services/product-design).

## Who this is for

Paid social suits products that can be explained to someone who was not looking for them. If your category is purely intent-driven and nobody discovers it by scrolling, [Google Ads](/services/google-ads) will usually be the better first channel.

It also requires an appetite for creative volume. If sign-off on a single ad takes three weeks, the channel cannot work as designed, and no amount of media skill compensates. That constraint is worth solving before the budget is.

And it needs a destination worth sending people to. Paid social is unusually good at exposing a weak landing experience, because it sends people who were not already convinced.
